Problem

Digital accelerators spend most of their energy moving numbers and multiplying them. Light passing through a structured medium performs a matrix-like transformation at almost no energy cost. The open problem is programming and training such a medium. This project asks whether a volume of nonlinear optical material, written by a shaped pump beam, can be trained as a classifier, with the training gradient computed by the light itself.

Deriving propagation by hand

Before writing any simulator, I derived how light propagates from first principles in Desmos. The idea is that any field can be written as a sum of tilted plane waves. Each plane wave travels without changing shape, only accumulating phase. So you decompose, phase-shift and recompose.

4. From this to the simulator. Put a material in the way, and between free-space steps the light also picks up phase from the local refractive index. Alternating the two gives the split-step Fourier beam-propagation method that runs the whole project. The adjoint pass is the same machinery run backwards. The error field is propagated from the detectors toward the input with the conjugate phases, and where forward and backward waves overlap, they tell each voxel which way to change.

Physical model

  • Device. A 32 × 32 × 108 µm Kerr medium. A 60 × 60 phase-and-amplitude metasurface shapes a pump beam whose intensity writes the index change Δn=n2I\Delta n = n_2 IΔn=n2​I. The pump's resolution sets 1.07 × 1.07 × 15.5 µm control voxels, giving 7,200 voxels to match the 7,200 metasurface controls. ∣Δn∣≲0.05|\Delta n| \lesssim 0.05∣Δn∣≲0.05.
  • Inference. The input image is encoded on the signal beam. Propagation is modelled by the paraxial wave equation
∂ψ∂z=i2k∇⊥2ψ+ik0 Δn(x,y,z) ψ\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z} = \frac{i}{2k}\nabla_\perp^2 \psi + i k_0\,\Delta n(x,y,z)\,\psi∂z∂ψ​=2ki​∇⊥2​ψ+ik0​Δn(x,y,z)ψ

solved with a split-step Fourier beam-propagation method (321 axial steps, with absorbing boundaries). Detector-region intensities give class scores, trained with a soft-target cross-entropy.

Learning with waves: the adjoint method

Brute-force gradients need two simulations per control. The adjoint method sends the output error backwards as a second field vvv. One forward and one backward pass then give the derivative with respect to every voxel:

∂L∂n=−2k0 Δz  Im ⁣(ψ∗v)\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial n} = -2k_0\,\Delta z\;\mathrm{Im}\!\left(\psi^{*} v\right)∂n∂L​=−2k0​ΔzIm(ψ∗v)

Validation. On a 60 × 64 test grid the adjoint gradient was compared with brute-force finite differences at all 3,840 voxels (7,680 extra simulations). The relative error was 0.078 % with correlation 0.9999997. A separate 60-probe finite-difference check gave a mean ratio of 0.99991 ± 0.0046.

Measuring the gradient with real detectors

Photodetectors measure intensity, not complex fields. If the forward and adjoint waves run at slightly different frequencies, their interference beats:

I(t)=∣ψ∣2+∣v∣2+2 Re(ψ∗v)cos⁡Δωt−2 Im(ψ∗v)sin⁡ΔωtI(t) = |\psi|^2 + |v|^2 + 2\,\mathrm{Re}(\psi^{*}v)\cos\Delta\omega t - 2\,\mathrm{Im}(\psi^{*}v)\sin\Delta\omega tI(t)=∣ψ∣2+∣v∣2+2Re(ψ∗v)cosΔωt−2Im(ψ∗v)sinΔωt

The sin⁡Δωt\sin\Delta\omega tsinΔωt quadrature is exactly the gradient term, so in principle the device can be trained in situ.

Respecting what the writer can do

Not every index pattern can be written by a pump beam. Instead of designing an ideal pattern and trying to reproduce it, each update is projected onto patterns the modelled writer can produce. Because the adjoint gradient is a product of two smooth waves, it naturally avoids most unreachable sharp features.

Limitations

This is a simulation feasibility study, not an optimised design and not hardware.

  • The material is idealised and lossless. Back-action of the pump and signal on the medium is neglected.
  • Under the assumed Kerr-writing mechanism, Δn≈0.05\Delta n \approx 0.05Δn≈0.05 would require roughly 2,200× the self-focusing critical power. The physical writing mechanism is the main open question.
  • An earlier 120-step result predates an absorber correction and is not comparable. The device aspect ratio (3.6) is still provisional.

Motivation

Low-power optical inference is useful where power, not silicon, limits computation, for example on long-duration space missions (the NJ Space Grant Consortium's interest). In this design the device also computes its own training gradient.
