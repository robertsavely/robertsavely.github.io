Goal

The course gave every team an overhead "IoT LiDAR" that published each robot's (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) over MQTT. In practice it was buggy: positions dropped out, lagged, or jumped across the arena. Most teams used it as-is. I wanted the robot to know where it was on its own, from six cheap ultrasonic sensors and a map, and to plan its own route around the buildings with enough clearance to be safe, without being slow.

That meant three problems: localisation (where am I?), planning (how do I get there?), and hardware (how do six sensors and two motors fit on one small board?). The plan was to split the work across two chips: one for sensing, localisation and routing, one dedicated to motor control.

Hardware: one pin per sensor

A Wemos D1 (ESP8266) has only a handful of usable GPIO pins. Six ultrasonic sensors normally need twelve (trigger + echo each), plus two motor signals and the display bus. Reading the sensor datasheet, I realised the trigger and echo phases never overlap in time. You send a 10 µs trigger pulse, then the sensor answers with an echo pulse. So one wire can do both: drive it as an output to trigger, then switch it to an input and time the echo. I spliced each sensor's TRIG and ECHO lines together onto a single data pin (Ultrasonic u1(D10, D10) … u6(D9, D9) in the firmware). That halved the pin count to six.

The sensors sit 60° apart around the chassis, 9.2 cm from the centre, and each sees a cone about 30° wide.

Localisation, part 1: what does one reading tell you?

A reading of rrr cm from a sensor pointing at angle θ\thetaθ doesn't give a point. It gives an arc: somewhere in that 30° cone, at distance ≈ rrr, there is a wall. So I tested every candidate pose with three concentric arcs per sensor:

  • the inner arc at r−δr - \deltar−δ must be in free space (otherwise the sensor would have hit something sooner),
  • the outer arc at r+δr + \deltar+δ must be inside a wall or outside the arena (otherwise it would have read further),
  • the measured arc at rrr sits between them, so the wall must cross the band.

A pose is plausible only if all six sensors' bands are consistent. That also handles noise: δ\deltaδ is the tolerance band (±5 cm in the solver), and a single wild reading makes a pose fail instead of silently skewing an average.

Localisation, part 2: every pose that fits

plotLogic.cpp searched for a valid pose by spiralling outward from the last known position, rotating at each point, and running the six arc tests concurrently (one std::async thread per sensor). Running that same rule exhaustively over the whole competition map, using the six readings hard-coded in the solver, shows what the data alone can and can't tell you:

Out of 1.5 million candidate poses, 0.17 % survive, but they fall into two mirror-image clusters: against the left wall facing in, and against the right wall facing back. Distances alone can't tell a symmetric arena apart, so the next step adds motion history.

Localisation, part 3: Markov + Monte Carlo

The robot's pose now depends on where it was a moment ago and how it moved. That's the Markov assumption, and it turns localisation into a recursive Bayes filter:

The integral is intractable over a continuous map, so test.cpp approximates the belief with particles: Monte Carlo localisation.

  1. Predict (motion model). Seed particles around the last pose: a quarter-circle ahead or behind, up to 5 units out, stretched in the direction of travel when the robot is moving. Motion noise ±0.1 is added.
  2. Weigh (sensor model). Each particle's weight is the fraction of sensors whose arc test passes: w=k/6w = k/6w=k/6.
  3. Resample with annealing. Draw new particles in proportion to w1/Tw^{1/T}w1/T, starting at T=10T = 10T=10 and cooling T←0.9TT \leftarrow 0.9TT←0.9T each round. Hot means explore broadly, cold means commit to the best hypotheses. That's the same idea as simulated annealing.
  4. Decide. Certainty =1/(σxσy)= 1/(\sigma_x\sigma_y)=1/(σx​σy​) of the particle cloud. The pose counts as found when certainty exceeds 300 and the mean pose has moved less than 1 unit and 0.1 rad over three consecutive rounds.

Run it yourself on the real competition map:

What the demo shows (and offline runs of the same logic confirm):

  • With the Markov prior (particles seeded around a last pose that's ~17 units off), it converges to within 0.3–2.5 units of the true position.
  • Seeded anywhere, it usually locks onto the wrong, symmetric location, the same ambiguity as the pose cloud above, so the motion model is required.
  • With a faulty sensor (one reading stuck at maximum range), the k/6k/6k/6 weighting degrades gracefully. It still lands within about a unit in some positions but drifts 9–21 units in others. The strict all-six rule in plotLogic.cpp would instead reject every pose. The choice of likelihood trades robustness against precision.

Filtering the IoT feed

For the runs where we fell back on the overhead tracker, the firmware wouldn't trust it blindly. A new position was rejected if it jumped more than 10 units in half a second and pointed away from the current direction of travel:

The robot then stopped and re-queried until the reading was physically consistent. This kinematic check removed the jumps.

Planning

The router is geometric instead of a grid search:

  1. Cast a straight line from the robot to the target and step along it.
  2. If it enters a building, identify which wall was hit, walk along it to both corners, and push each corner outward by a clearance margin (2 units in the simulator, 5 in the final firmware).
  3. Discard corners outside the arena or inside another building, go to the one closer to the target, and repeat from there.
c∗=arg⁡min⁡c ∈ {c1,c2} valid∥c−g∥c^{*} = \arg\min_{c \,\in\, \{c_1, c_2\} \text{ valid}} \lVert c - g \rVertc∗=argc∈{c1​,c2​} validmin​∥c−g∥

That hugs buildings as tightly as the margin allows, so it's short and fast, while the clearance keeps the chassis and its sensor cones off the walls.

Control (the second chip's job)

Steering uses the geometry directly. The cross product of the planned direction and the actual direction of motion gives the sign and size of the heading error. Above 45° the robot turns in place, for a time scaled from a measured full-rotation time of 1.865 s. Below that, it drives forward and trims one motor by ±5 based on the perpendicular distance from the planned line, stopping within 10 units of each target. In the C++ planner these motor commands were stubs, deliberately left for a separate motor-control chip.

Known issues

  • The full particle filter ran on a laptop (test.cpp, visualised in gnuplot). The competition firmware (HAILMARY.ino, named for a reason) used the overhead feed with the kinematic filter, plus the onboard router and steering.
  • test.cpp's weighting only checked the arena boundary ("add the building intersections later"). The demo above uses the full building-aware arc test from plotLogic.cpp.
  • The original resampler copied particles exactly, which causes particle depletion. The demo's roughening option adds the small jitter that fixes it.
  • The firmware's corner-distance comparison used ^2, which is XOR in C++, not squaring.
