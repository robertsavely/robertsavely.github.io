Not quantum mechanics, and not investment advice. "Path integral" is the Feynman–Dirac idea of summing over every possible history, applied to stock prices. Result: the model's point forecasts did not beat a random walk, but its uncertainty estimates were well calibrated.

The question

Most forecasting asks what will the price be? This project asks a different question, following Soleymani & Paquet (2022): of all the paths the price could take, which are most believable, and what do they say on average?

The idea, one formula at a time

1. Work in log-price. With z(t)=ln⁡S(t)z(t) = \ln S(t)z(t)=lnS(t), a return is just a difference: Δz=ln⁡(St+1/St)\Delta z = \ln(S_{t+1}/S_t)Δz=ln(St+1​/St​). Returns add up over time and are roughly Gaussian.

2. A drift and a volatility describe each day. Over a small step, the classic model of a stock is

Δzt=μt Δt+σt Δt  εt,εt∼N(0,1)\Delta z_t = \mu_t\,\Delta t + \sigma_t\,\sqrt{\Delta t}\;\varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal N(0,1)Δzt​=μt​Δt+σt​Δt​εt​,εt​∼N(0,1)

where μt\mu_tμt​ is where the price is "trying" to go and σt\sigma_tσt​ is how noisy it is. The Bayesian network's job is to predict μt\mu_tμt​ and σt\sigma_tσt​ for each future day, with uncertainty.

3. That makes every step a probability. Rearranging gives εt\varepsilon_tεt​, so the chance of one particular step is a Gaussian:

p(Δzt)∝exp⁡ ⁣(−(Δzt−μtΔt)22σt2 Δt)p(\Delta z_t) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\Delta z_t - \mu_t\Delta t)^2}{2\sigma_t^2\,\Delta t}\right)p(Δzt​)∝exp(−2σt2​Δt(Δzt​−μt​Δt)2​)

4. A path's probability is the product of its steps, and products of exponentials become a sum: the action.

p(path)∝∏tp(Δzt)=exp⁡ ⁣(−S[z]),S[z]=∑t(z˙t−μt)22σt2 Δtp(\text{path}) \propto \prod_t p(\Delta z_t) = \exp\!\big(-\mathcal S[z]\big), \qquad \mathcal S[z] = \sum_t \frac{(\dot z_t - \mu_t)^2}{2\sigma_t^2}\,\Delta tp(path)∝t∏​p(Δzt​)=exp(−S[z]),S[z]=t∑​2σt2​(z˙t​−μt​)2​Δt

This is the same structure as Feynman's path integral: each history gets weight e−Se^{-\mathcal S}e−S, where the action S\mathcal SS measures how far the path strays from the expected motion. Paths that move the way the BNN expects get large weights, and erratic ones get tiny weights.

5. You can't enumerate every path, so sample plausible ones with a GAN and reweight them.

z^(T)=∑kwk zk(T)∑kwk,wk=e−S[zk]/τ,ESS=(∑kwk)2∑kwk2\hat z(T) = \frac{\sum_k w_k\, z_k(T)}{\sum_k w_k}, \qquad w_k = e^{-\mathcal S[z_k]/\tau}, \qquad \text{ESS} = \frac{\left(\sum_k w_k\right)^2}{\sum_k w_k^2}z^(T)=∑k​wk​∑k​wk​zk​(T)​,wk​=e−S[zk​]/τ,ESS=∑k​wk2​(∑k​wk​)2​

This is importance sampling. The same weights give quantiles, P(up)P(\text{up})P(up) and tail risk. The effective sample size (ESS) shows how many paths contribute. If one path takes all the weight, ESS → 1 and the forecast is fragile. The temperature τ\tauτ controls that: τ→∞\tau \to \inftyτ→∞ is a plain average of the GAN's paths, and τ→0\tau \to 0τ→0 trusts only the single most likely path.

At 5-minute resolution it failed:

5-minute experiment: the expected price slides in a straight line while the real price goes sideways.(5_minute_intervals.ipynb)

When the generator is biased, the weighting has no good paths to choose between, so the GAN's bias becomes the forecast. Small per-step errors add up linearly over the horizon. This is why the rebuild calibrates the generated paths.

QuantumPath (Oct 2026): rebuild and evaluation

Rebuilt as a tested system with AI coding assistance. It runs a parameter search, walk-forward evaluation, HPC sharding and a dashboard. Changes from the paper, each motivated by what broke above:

ChangeWhy
Calibrate generated paths to the data's per-step mean and stdRemoves the generator bias seen in the 5-minute run
Cumulative weighting ∏s≤tp(Δzs)\prod_{s\le t}p(\Delta z_s)∏s≤t​p(Δzs​) instead of per-dayIt's the actual path-integral measure, not a day-by-day approximation
Temperature τ\tauτ searchableLets the data decide how much to trust the weighting
Report five baselines every timeA random walk is the standard benchmark for weekly returns

Results: 16 configurations, 288 out-of-sample forecasts

No configuration beat the random walk. The best came in at 1.045× its error, and the BNN-alone and GAN-alone baselines did about as well as the full model. Plotting every individual forecast shows why:

There is no useful tilt. If anything it leans the wrong way: the correlation between predicted and realised returns is r=−0.20r = -0.20r=−0.20, and the direction was right 45.8 % of the time. The 288 forecasts share only 18 distinct outcomes (9 stocks × 2 test windows), so this isn't evidence of anything systematic, only that there's no edge to find here. But the 90 % intervals contained the outcome 91.7 % of the time. The uncertainty estimates were calibrated even though the direction was not predictable. A single live forecast looks like this:

Conclusions

  • Drift is small next to noise. At weekly horizons μt≪σt\mu_t \ll \sigma_tμt​≪σt​, so the action is dominated by the noise term and reweighting paths does not recover a direction.
  • Volatility was predictable; drift was not. Volatility clusters over time, which is why the 90 % intervals held.
  • The generator bias was visible early. It showed in one 5-minute plot, months before the HPC system was built.
  • The baseline decided the result. Without the random-walk comparison, the forecast fans would look like a working model.

The project has since moved to supply-chain exposure: modelling which companies are sensitive to which commodities (10-K counterparties from SEC EDGAR, commodity betas with false-discovery control). That question depends on published company structure rather than price direction.
