The brief

We were given a 5-second audio clip and asked to build an analog front end that amplifies it by at least 10 dB and splits it between two speakers: lows to a woofer, highs to a midrange driver. The middle frequency must be attenuated by at least 12 dB in both outputs. Teammate: Xiangxi Wang.

The finished build: five LM741s on ±10 V.(final presentation)

Step 1: frequency content

An FFT of the clip in MATLAB (FFT.m) showed it is essentially three pure tones:

MATLAB FFT: tones at 41.2 Hz (woofer), 493.8 Hz (must be rejected) and 3,322.5 Hz (midrange).(final report, Fig. 1.0)

So the requirements are: the low-pass must pass 41.2 Hz and reject 493.8 Hz by ≥ 12 dB. The high-pass must pass 3,322.5 Hz and reject 493.8 Hz by ≥ 12 dB.

Step 2: optimise the filter, cutoff and order together

A Butterworth filter of order nnn and cutoff fcf_cfc​ has magnitude

Each requirement becomes an equation in (fc,n)(f_c, n)(fc​,n). "12 dB down" means ∣H∣=10−12/20=0.251|H| = 10^{-12/20} = 0.251∣H∣=10−12/20=0.251 at 493.8 Hz. "Pass the wanted tone" was set as ∣H∣≥0.99|H| \ge 0.99∣H∣≥0.99. I solved both for nnn as a function of fcf_cfc​ in Desmos and read off where they cross:

The result: order 2 is the smallest integer order that satisfies both constraints. Holding n=2n = 2n=2, the cutoff was set at the limit of the rejection constraint, 251 Hz for the low-pass and 971 Hz for the high-pass. That gives each speaker the widest passband a 2nd-order filter can afford while still meeting the −12 dB spec. The Analog Devices Filter Wizard confirmed both designs:

Low-pass: −3 dB at 251 Hz, −12 dB at 494 Hz.(final report, Fig. 1.1)High-pass: −3 dB at 971 Hz, −12 dB at 494 Hz.(final presentation)

Simscape model: amplifier → two unity-gain Sallen-Key filters → buffers → 4 Ω and 3.35 Ω loads.(final report, Fig. 1.4)

Step 4: build, measure, and explain the gap

The breadboard used the closest parts available, measured individually:

High-pass stage.(final presentation)Low-pass stage.(final presentation)

The woofer channel worked immediately. The midrange channel was silent. Probing node by node with an oscilloscope, using the working channel as a reference, found a dead LM741 buffer and then a broken jumper to the speaker. After the repairs it played, but sounded deeper than the simulation. Toggle As built below to see why:

The 100 nF capacitors measured about 114–121 nF. In the high-pass, f0∝1/C1C2f_0 \propto 1/\sqrt{C_1C_2}f0​∝1/C1​C2​​, so ~17 % extra capacitance pulls the cutoff from 970 Hz to about 826 Hz. The mid tone is then only 9.5 dB down instead of 12 dB, and the signal-to-interference ratio at the midrange speaker falls from 11.3 dB to 8.7 dB. This matches the deeper sound we heard. The low-pass barely moved (251 → 258 Hz) because its resistor substitutions roughly cancelled its capacitor error, which is why that channel matched the simulation. Our report attributed the gap to component tolerance and breadboard parasitics. The calculation above puts a number on the tolerance part.

Changes for a second build

  • Choose capacitor values from measured stock first, then solve for resistors. Resistors are easier to trim than capacitors.
  • Add a small trim on the high-pass. Its cutoff is the one sitting right on the edge of the spec.
  • As the report notes, give the high-pass path some gain so the two speakers are balanced. The input clip's 41 Hz tone is 7 dB louder than the 3.3 kHz tone.
