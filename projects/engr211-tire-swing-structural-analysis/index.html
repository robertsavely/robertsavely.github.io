Problem

ENGR 211's final project was a backyard playground for 5–12-year-olds, analysed for safety under ASTM F1148 loads. My element in our five-person team was a three-rope tire swing hung from a red-maple top beam on an A-frame. The question was how small the members could be while every one still met its target factor of safety under a swinging load.

Loads

The worst case is the bottom of the swing after release from θmax⁡=π/4\theta_{\max} = \pi/4θmax​=π/4. That gives pendulum tension T=mg(3−2cos⁡θ)T = mg(3 - 2\cos\theta)T=mg(3−2cosθ), written in the workbook as

Fdown=W+2 mg (1−cos⁡θmax⁡),W=3 riders×140 lb×2+tire+ropesF_{\text{down}} = W + 2\,m g\,(1-\cos\theta_{\max}), \qquad W = 3\ \text{riders}\times 140\ \text{lb}\times 2 + \text{tire} + \text{ropes}Fdown​=W+2mg(1−cosθmax​),W=3 riders×140 lb×2+tire+ropes

That's 1,372 lb from an 840 lb static rider load. The ropes share it three ways, giving a factor of safety of 4.37 against 2,000 lb-rated HMPE rope (target ≈ 4).

The hand derivation behind the rope check: energy conservation from the release angle to the bottom of the arc.(final presentation)

The goal: least material that is still safe

The centre of my workbook is an optimisation: minimise the volume of wood in the frame while every member stays inside its safety target. Oversizing is easy and always "safe", but it wastes material, weight and cost. Undersizing fails. The right answer is the smallest member that still meets its factor of safety, not the biggest one that fits.

The top beam uses a triangular section (a right-isosceles triangle of side sss: base 2s\sqrt2 s2​s, height s/2s/\sqrt2s/2​). With the shape fixed, the remaining work was to size each dimension.

Writing each requirement as an equation

I wrote each design condition as a residual that equals zero at the optimum in a "Formula Box" column, then solved for the zero. For the top beam:

I=bh336=s472,c=h3,M=PL3+wL28,r(s)=McI−MORFoSI = \frac{b h^3}{36} = \frac{s^4}{72},\quad c = \frac{h}{3},\quad M = \frac{P L}{3} + \frac{w L^2}{8},\qquad r(s) = \frac{M c}{I} - \frac{\text{MOR}}{\text{FoS}}I=36bh3​=72s4​,c=3h​,M=3PL​+8wL2​,r(s)=IMc​−FoSMOR​

with modulus of rupture 13,400 psi, FoS 3 and span 24 ft.

Shear and moment diagrams for the top beam, the triangular-section inertia I = bh³/36, and the FoS condition, worked by hand before going into the spreadsheet.(final presentation)

Beam: trading safety against wood

The wood in the beam grows with s2s^2s2, but its strength grows with s3s^3s3. So the safety factor rises faster than the material does, and there is exactly one side length where the beam reaches FoS 3. Anything smaller is unsafe; anything larger is wood carrying no purpose. Drag the side length:

The minimum is s* ≈ 8.09 in: about 5.45 ft³ (≈ 207 lb) of red maple against 12 ft³ (456 lb) for the 12-in section the CAD model was drawn with. That is 55 % less wood for the same FoS-3 target.

Legs: the angle where neither failure mode is over-built

The A-frame legs have two ways to fail. A shallow leg takes the load across the grain, in shear, where wood is weakest. A steep leg takes it along the grain, in compression. The post must be big enough for whichever is worse. The workbook's second residual (cell K40) finds the angle where the two needs are equal, which is where the post can be smallest:

A-frame leg: compression and shear equilibrium, each with its own factor of safety.(final presentation)

Result

Sized this way, each member runs close to its own target instead of carrying large hidden margins:

The ropes sit at FoS 4.37 against a target of about 4. The beam at s∗s^*s∗ and the legs at φ∗\varphi^*φ∗ sit exactly on their targets. For comparison, the 12-in beam drawn in CAD would run at only a third of its capacity.

Known issues

  • One shear formula uses COS(45). Excel reads that as 45 radians, not degrees. The rest of the sheet correctly uses PI()/4.
  • The workbook's recorded bending factor (13.8) and the team presentation's (7.57) disagree. Both are preserved as found.
  • Anchor-plate and bolt-shear rows were listed but never computed.

Approach

Writing each requirement as an equation and solving for its zero replaced guess-and-check. It turns "is this safe?" into "what is the least material that is safe?". Later projects use the same approach.
